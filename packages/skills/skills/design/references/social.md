## What A Post Is

A post is met mid-scroll on a phone, beside photos of people and things, and gets about a second before the thumb moves on. It is closer to a poster than to a web page: one message, seen at a glance, at a fraction of its pixel size. Most weak generated posts are a landing page fitted into a square, with a heading, a paragraph, a bulleted list, a button, and a logo, all at web sizes.

Decide the one thing a viewer should take from it in that second, such as a number, a date, a claim, a face, or a product. Everything else on the canvas supports that thing or goes into the caption, which carries the detail and the call to action. A story has no caption: its detail goes on the next story or behind a link sticker. Give each feed image alt text that repeats the text on the canvas, where the platform allows it.

## Canvas

Use the platform's size; confirm it against the platform's current guidance when you can search, since these change.

- **Instagram and Facebook feed:** 1080×1350 (4:5 portrait) takes the most screen in the feed and is the default. Use 1080×1080 when the content is square by nature. The Instagram profile grid shows feed posts in a narrower tile, so keep text off the outer side edges.
- **Stories and Reels covers:** 1080×1920. The interface covers the top, the bottom, and the right side: keep text and faces clear of about 250px at the top, 480px at the bottom, 140px on the right, and 60px on the left.
- **Link previews:** about 1200×630. The platform prints the page title under the image, so the image should not repeat it.

Keep a margin of at least 64px on a 1080px-wide canvas for everything that must be read.

## Text Size

A 1080px-wide post is shown about 390 points wide on a phone, roughly a third of its size. Text set at web sizes on the canvas turns into captions nobody can read.

The one fixed value is the floor: on a 1080px-wide canvas, text that must be read is never below 36px, since it shows at about 13 points on a phone. Everything above the floor follows from the hierarchy, planned before any text is set:

1. **List every piece of text** the canvas will carry, such as the message, the date, the price, the handle, and the slide number. Move what the viewer does not need in the first second to the caption.
2. **Assign each piece to a level:** the main message, the details, and, when needed, a signature level for the handle and the slide number. Pieces on the same level share one style, so the number of styles equals the number of levels. A new treatment for one piece, such as tracked capitals for the brand or an italic lead-in, is a new level the viewer has to rank.
3. **Size by proportion.** The main message is the only large text; scale it to the canvas, filling the width when it is short, and make it several times the size of the details. Set the details as small as reads comfortably, near the floor. The signature level is no larger than the details and quieter in weight or color. When a date or a price is set halfway between the details and the message, there are two messages.
4. **State the plan** with the decisions: each level, the pieces on it, and its style.

Keep the canvas to as few words as the message allows: the main message readable in a glance, and the details in a short block. When a post or a slide needs a paragraph, the paragraph goes in the caption or becomes another slide.

- Use the heavier weights of a face, open counters, and few lines. Hairline strokes, fine serifs, and thin rules blur in the platform's compression.

## Composition

- **One focal element, large.** Give the thing from What A Post Is about half the canvas or more: the number at display size, the product photo cropped close, the face filling the frame. A post where every element is medium-sized reads as nothing from a feed.
- **Use the content as the image.** A real photo, a product screenshot cropped to the part that matters, a chart with one line highlighted, or type alone. A post with none of these is a type poster, which works when the type is large and set with care. Never stand in a crude drawing or a stock-like gradient for an image you do not have.
- **No interface parts.** A drawn button cannot be tapped, a navigation bar has nowhere to go, and chips, tick lists, and shadowed cards are web-page residue. State the action in words, such as the date and place, and put the link where the platform makes it tappable: the caption where links work there, the profile link for an Instagram feed post, or a link sticker in a story.
- **Compose for the frame, not a card in it.** Let the content reach the edges with a margin. A small rounded card floating in the middle of a gradient wastes most of the canvas.
- **One reading path.** Read top to bottom, the post goes from the main message to the details, each once. Keep the details together in one block in the order people need them, such as what, when, where, how much, and how to join, as lines of plain text. A brand in one corner, a city in another, a date in a third, and a price in the fourth make the viewer hunt. The signature level, such as the handle and the slide number, sits together in one place, not split across corners.
- **No lines, chips, or monospace.** A post has no table to rule and nothing to select. Rules, bordered pills, and monospaced labels make it look like an interface.
- **Asymmetry over centering.** Centered type in the middle of the canvas is the default of every template tool. Align to one edge and let the focal element take the opposite side, unless the content is a single short symmetric line.
- **Brand small and fixed.** The logo or handle sits small, in the same corner on every post. It is how a series is recognized, not the message.
- **Contrast against the feed.** The feed is busy and mostly photographic. Strong value contrast and a few flat colors stand out more than a gradient or a busy texture, which reads as more feed.

## Text Over Images

- Put text where the photo is calm, such as sky, a wall, or out-of-focus space, and crop the photo to create that area when it has none.
- When no area is calm enough, lay a solid or semi-opaque band behind the text, or darken the image under it with a gradient that runs from the text's side. Do not add a glow or heavy outline to the letters.
- Measure contrast against the busiest part of the image behind the text, not its average.
- Keep text off faces and off the product.

## Carousels

A carousel is a sequence read by swiping. The first slide decides whether anyone swipes.

- **The cover carries the promise only:** what the viewer gets by swiping, as a specific claim or question. Do not spend it on the brand or an introduction.
- **One point per slide,** at the same text sizes as a single post. A slide that needs a paragraph is two slides.
- **Hold the layout.** Keep margins, the type positions, the text sizes, and the color roles identical on every slide, so each swipe reads as the next page of the same thing and only the content changes. Number the slides when order matters.
- **No label above each headline.** A small uppercase or colored line such as a step and a topic over every slide's headline is the generated label repeated five times, and a level of its own. Put the step number in the headline or with the slide number, and let the headline name the topic.
- **Lead into the next slide** with the content, such as a step number or a question the next slide answers, rather than with a "swipe" arrow on every slide.
- **End on the payoff,** such as the summary, the result, or the one action, and keep the action in words.
- Each slide may be shared or seen alone, so each one must still make sense with its slide number and the brand mark.

## Series And Templates

When the work is a template or a recurring series, such as a weekly schedule, a quote, or a product announcement, design the system: which parts are fixed, which change, and the longest and shortest content each slot must hold. Test it with the real extremes, such as the longest class name, a week with one class and a week with twelve, before calling it done. Seen together on a profile grid, the posts should read as one account through fixed placement, type, and a small palette, while each post differs in its focal element.

## Rendering

- Build the post as an HTML page whose body is exactly the canvas size, with no scrolling and no outside margin.
- Render it with a headless browser at that viewport and a device scale factor of 1, so the output is exactly the canvas in pixels.
- Before capturing, wait for the page's fonts to finish loading and for every image to decode. A capture taken early shows the fallback font and still looks finished.
- Export PNG for type, flat color, and screenshots, and high-quality JPEG for photographs. Use sRGB.
- Save each slide of a carousel as its own file, numbered in order.

## Social Failures

These are the patterns that make a generated post read as generated, in addition to the general ones:

- A landing page in a square: a heading, a paragraph, a button, and a footer of links or contact details.
- Text at web sizes: 16–32px type on a 1080px canvas.
- Three or more large lines competing: a big headline, a big date, and a big price.
- A different type treatment for each piece of text: tracked caps, an italic lead-in, a display serif, a colored number.
- Everything centered in a floating card on a gradient or a blurred photo.
- A list of features, each with an emoji or an icon.
- A headline with a single word in the accent color, a glow, or gradient text.
- Five or more colors, or a different style on every slide of a carousel.
- Stickers, bursts, and badges added for energy that carry no information.

## Checking A Post

- View it at about 390px wide, the size of a phone feed, and again at about a quarter of its width, the size of a grid tile. At the first, every word must read; at the second, the main message must still read.
- Look at it for one second, look away, and say what it said. When that is not the one thing from What A Post Is, enlarge it or cut what competes with it.
- Read the canvas aloud, labels included. When it takes more than a few seconds, move text to the caption or split the slide.
- Check the render against the hierarchy plan: every piece of text on its level, one style per level, and no piece in a style of its own. A rendered style the plan does not name is a level added by accident.
- Check that each fact appears once. A label, a caption, and a progress marker that all repeat the same step or number are three claims on one second of attention.
- Count the colors in use.
- For a carousel, view the slides side by side: the layout should hold still while the content changes.
- Check the text against the platform's covered areas for its format.
