# Branding

A brand identity is a system of decisions that all follow from one strategic idea: who the brand is for, what it stands for, and how it differs from the alternatives. The logo, colors, type, words, and images are expressions of that idea. When each piece can be traced back to it, the pieces look and sound like one brand; when they cannot, they are a collection of nice assets.

Generated branding fails in predictable ways: it jumps to a logo and palette before any strategy, describes the personality with adjectives every competitor would claim ("innovative, trustworthy, modern"), writes taglines that fit any company, picks colors and fonts that match the category average or the generator's own average, repeats the same copy patterns everywhere, and presents finished-looking mockups with no rules behind them. Work in order, from understanding to strategy to expression, and test each step against the competitors before moving on.

## Existing Brand First

For a refresh or an extension, audit what exists before changing anything: which assets people recognize, such as a color, a shape, a name, a sound, or a phrase, and which are inconsistent or broken. Keep and strengthen what is recognized. Replace it only when asked to, or when the audit shows it works against the strategy, and say what recognition the change gives up.

## Procedure

1. **Discovery.** Establish the business, the offer, the audience, the competitors, and the constraints, as described in Discovery.
2. **Strategy.** Write the positioning, the brand idea, and the personality, as described in Strategy.
3. **Directions.** At concept stage, develop two or three directions that interpret the strategy differently, as described in Directions.
4. **Verbal identity.** Name when needed, tagline, voice, and messaging, as described in Verbal Identity.
5. **Visual identity.** Logo, color, type, imagery, and graphic elements, as described in Visual Identity.
6. **Applications.** Apply the system to real touchpoints, as described in Applications.
7. **Check.** Follow Verification below.
8. **Deliver.** Write the guidelines and the presentation. Read [references/deliverables.md](references/deliverables.md) before writing brand guidelines or preparing a brand presentation.

Not every request needs every step. A tagline alone still needs the positioning; a color refresh still needs the personality and the competitor map. Do the steps the request depends on, and state which upstream decisions you inferred rather than received.

## Discovery

- **The offer:** what is sold, to whom, at what price level, and why someone chooses it over the alternatives.
- **The audience:** a specific group with a specific need, not "everyone" or "businesses". Include who decides and who uses, when they differ.
- **Competitors:** collect five to ten, including indirect alternatives. For each, record its name, its claim, its main colors, its type style, and its tone. When you can browse the web, open each competitor's site or social profile to record its colors and type; a search result listing does not show them. For a competitor whose colors you still cannot find, write that against its name. Then map where they cluster. The cluster is what this brand must not look or sound like.
- **Constraints:** existing assets, budget for licensed fonts and photography, markets and languages, legal or industry requirements, and the touchpoints that matter most.

Ask the client the questions whose answers would change the direction, such as the audience or the price level. For the rest, state assumptions. Write the result as a short brief and confirm it before strategy.

## Strategy

- **Positioning.** One sentence: for [audience] who [need], [brand] is the [category or frame of reference] that [main difference], because [reason to believe]. The difference must be true, matter to the audience, and be something competitors do not already claim.
- **Brand idea.** One short sentence that everything else expresses, such as an attitude, a belief, or a promise. It guides decisions: "Would the brand idea do this?" should have an answer.
- **Personality.** Define it as positions on scales between opposites, such as serious to playful, classic to experimental, exclusive to accessible, restrained to expressive, with a reason for each position. Then name, for each trait, the nearby misreading it must avoid, such as arrogance for a confident brand. Keep that contrast in the strategy; it is not a pattern for the brand's own copy.
- **Values,** only when they change behavior, such as what the brand refuses to do. Values every company claims, such as quality and integrity, add nothing.

Test every strategy statement by asking whether a named competitor could sign it unchanged. If one could, it does not differentiate; rewrite it.

## Directions

At concept stage, present two or three directions before finishing any of them. Each direction is a different interpretation of the same strategy, with its own name, a one-sentence idea, and a mood: a few key frames showing type, color, imagery, and a sketch of the logo route together. Directions differ in idea, not merely in color. Finish the chosen direction only.

When the client has already chosen a direction, or the request is for a single element, skip this step.

## Verbal Identity

- **Naming.** When the brand needs a name, treat naming as its own task: generate widely across kinds of names, screen against the strategy, and check pronunciation, meaning in the target languages, domains, and existing trademarks in the category.
- **Tagline.** Generate at least fifteen across kinds: descriptive (what it is), benefit (what you get), imperative (what to do), and provocative (what it believes). Choose for clarity first, then memorability. A tagline that needs explaining has failed. Reject anything that fits a competitor equally well, and these patterns: "Empowering…", "Your partner in…", "Redefining…", "X, simplified", "Where X meets Y", "The future of X", and three adjectives in a row.
- **Voice.** Three or four principles, each derived from the personality. For each, rewrite the same real sentence, such as an error message, a welcome email, or a social post, in the brand's voice and in a nearby wrong voice, so the difference is concrete. State how tone shifts by situation, such as more direct in errors and warmer in onboarding, while the voice stays the same.
- **Messaging.** A one-line description, a 25-word description, a 50- to 75-word description, and three to five key messages, each with its proof.
- **Generated copy patterns.** Generated copy repeats a few constructions until they mark it: contrasts in the form "X, not Y" or "Less X. More Y.", two-fragment slogans, lists of three joined by middots, and a small uppercase label above every headline. Use each at most once across the whole identity, and only where it is the clearest way to say the thing. Write each line as a plain sentence first, and keep that version when it says the same.

## Visual Identity

Derive each element from the strategy and the competitor map, and be able to say for each why it fits the brand idea and how it differs from the cluster.

- **Logo.** Treat the logo as its own design task.
  - **Existing logo.** When the brand keeps its existing logo, change only what the request needs, such as a small-size or one-color version, and check every version you change at 16 and 32px, in one color, and reversed.
  - **Concepts.** For a new mark, write at least twenty concepts in words before drawing. Swap each competitor's name into each concept to identify shared category claims, and record the specific construction that could distinguish the mark. Shared benefits do not require an exclusive concept: discard a concept when neither its idea nor its proposed execution offers a meaningful distinction.
  - **Routes.** Develop three routes from the surviving concepts that differ in idea, built from precise geometry rather than freehand drawing, in black first, and compare their rendered silhouettes, lettering, and negative space with competitors. Check each at 16 and 32px, in one color, and reversed, and draw a simplified small version when details merge or vanish. At most one route may be built on the name's initial letter, and it must change the letter to carry an idea; an initial alone teaches nothing about a new name, and a rounded corner, a cut terminal, or a changed weight does not change it enough. When the mark is a lettermark or monogram, every route uses the letters, so each must change them to carry a different idea. A literal category image, such as a coffee bean for a roaster or a wheat stalk for a bakery, needs a distinctive construction; reject a generic picture that any competitor could use unchanged.
  - **Lettering.** When a route includes lettering, review every letter pair at the intended sizes and record each pair's adjustment, including zero, the overall tracking, the sizes reviewed, and why unchanged spacing was kept.
  - **Final files.** Final logo files contain no live text: convert lettering to outlines, or construct it as paths, so the logo does not depend on installed fonts. With Node and a local copy of the font file, run `npm install --prefix scripts harfbuzzjs@^1.6.4` from the skill directory so Node can resolve the dependency beside the script, then run [scripts/outline-wordmark.mjs](scripts/outline-wordmark.mjs): `node scripts/outline-wordmark.mjs Font.ttf "AVIVA" --out wordmark.svg --tracking=-10 --pair AV=-20`. It applies the font's kerning, the tracking, and each pair adjustment in thousandths of an em, writes the outlined paths with a `viewBox` fitted to the letters, and prints each pair's kerning and adjustment for the spacing record. Use a positive `--size`. Write negative spacing values with an equals sign, as in `--tracking=-10`. Add `--variation wght=600` only for a variable font with a `wght` axis. Pair adjustments apply to every matching pair of shaped clusters. Default ligatures stay joined; use `--features=-liga` when separate letters and their pair adjustments are needed. Other conversion tools can preserve kerning too; check the spacing and the fitted `viewBox` in the render. When the lettering cannot be outlined or constructed, label the file as a working file that still needs outlining.
- **Color.** A primary brand color chosen for distinctiveness in the category, a neutral range for text and backgrounds, an accent when needed, and functional colors for status in digital products. Define the proportions of use, since proportion is as recognizable as hue. Give values in HEX and RGB, and provide CMYK and spot-color matches only as starting points that a print professional must confirm. Check text contrast for every approved pairing: at least 4.5:1 for body text and 3:1 for large text and interface components, per WCAG AA. Logotypes, purely decorative text, and incidental text in photographs are exempt.
- **Generated defaults.** Generated identities converge on the same few palettes regardless of category: an off-white or cream ground with an orange, rust, terracotta, copper, or clay accent; indigo or violet for anything technical; a near-black or slate ground with one bright accent; and a serif display face over a neutral sans. Choose one of these only when the competitor map shows it is unclaimed and a specific part of the strategy calls for it, and write that reason in the guidelines. "Warm", "earthy", "crafted", and "honest" are not reasons; most generated brands claim them. When competitor colors could not be found, label the primary color provisional, and keep it away from these palettes and their near neighbors, such as oxblood, ochre, and brick.
- **Typography.** One or two families with distinct roles, chosen for the personality and with licenses covering every use the brand needs: web, app, print, video, and office documents. Name a free or system fallback for the documents people outside the design team will make.
- **Imagery.** Define the photography or illustration style by its subject, light, color treatment, framing, and what it avoids, with examples. Choose one style of iconography with a consistent stroke and corner.
- **Graphic elements.** One or two distinctive devices derived from the logo or the brand idea, such as a shape, a pattern, a crop, or a layout rule, that make materials recognizable without the logo.
- **Motion and sound,** when the brand lives on screen: the easing character, pace, and a sonic logo if any, following the personality.

## Applications

Apply the system to the touchpoints that matter most for this brand, such as a website hero, a social post, a business card, an app icon, a slide, packaging, signage, or an email signature. Render them; describing an application is not applying it. Applications test the system: when an element cannot handle a real case, such as a long headline, a busy photo, or a small format, fix the rule, not just the mockup.

- Build every application from the system's own assets: the logo file, never the name retyped in a font; the approved colors, type roles, and graphic elements. An application that departs from a rule is either an error or a missing rule; fix one or the other.
- Show physical items, such as packaging, signage, a vehicle, or merchandise, on the object's real shape at a believable scale: place the artwork on a photograph or mockup template of the object, or generate the scene with an image model when one is available. A flat colored rectangle labeled with the item's name is not a mockup, and neither is the flat print artwork alone. Without those, draw a flat-sided object, such as a pouch, box, carton, card, or sign panel, yourself: build it from precise geometry, with the artwork on its faces, and add little more than a seal, a fold, or one soft shadow. Do not draw organic or curved objects, such as a jar, bottle, garment, bag with handles, vehicle, or food, in SVG or CSS; they read as blobs and cost the identity more than a missing mockup. For those, deliver the print artwork labeled as artwork and state that the mockup was not produced.
- Use the brand's real content: product names, prices, screens, and data, with personal or confidential records replaced by realistic invented ones. When the product does not exist yet, write content specific enough that it could only belong to this brand, such as realistic records from its domain; generic interface panels and placeholder copy make every brand look the same.

## Verification

- **Category test.** Place the identity beside the competitors. It should be recognizably different from the cluster.
- **Logo-free test.** Cover the logo on several applications. They should still look like the same brand, through color, type, imagery, and graphic elements.
- **Trace test.** For each element, state which part of the strategy it expresses. Cut or change any element with no answer.
- **Cliché test.** Read every line of text, and compare the visuals against the clichés of the category. Replace what any competitor could have produced.
- **Copy pattern count.** Search every line of copy, in the guidelines, the presentation, and the applications, for ", not ", for "Less X. More Y." pairs, for two-fragment slogans, for middot lists, and for uppercase labels above headlines. Count each pattern; when one appears more than once, rewrite all but the clearest as plain sentences, then count again.
- **Look at the renders.** Open every rendered image and describe what it actually shows, part by part, before judging it. Missing, broken, or vanished elements are easy to miss when checking against what the code was meant to draw. View each logo file and application at its final size and confirm every letter and part is whole and clear of the edges.
- **Report what you saw.** For each check, write in the verification notes what you measured or saw, and list every defect found, fixed or not. A check reported only as passed was not run.
- **Consistency test.** View all applications side by side. They should apply the same rules for type, color proportion, spacing, and imagery.
- **Accessibility.** Text contrast of approved color pairings against the WCAG AA thresholds in Color, exempting logotypes and decorative text, legibility of the typefaces at the sizes they will be used, and the logo at its minimum size.
- **Clearance.** You cannot clear names, taglines, or logos legally. State what was checked, such as domains and an informal search, and that trademark clearance by a qualified person is needed before adoption, along with a native-speaker check in each target market.

When applications cannot be rendered, say so and name what was not checked.
